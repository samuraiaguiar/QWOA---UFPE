\subsection{Extended benchmark over windows, sizes, depths, and seeds}

The extended benchmark provides the main numerical evidence, within the small
and moderate feasible spaces considered here, that feasible-graph geometry can
affect performance beyond the illustrative five-asset example. Table~\ref{tab:aggregate-depth} aggregates the best result obtained within each
instance--method--depth group, after optimizing over random initializations and,
for the penalty-QAOA baseline, over the scanned penalty strengths. The Johnson and lightly weighted
Johnson mixers obtain larger \(P_\star\) and smaller normalized gaps than the
complete/Grover mixer. The penalty-QAOA baseline improves with depth, but
remains below the best feasible-walk methods in the tested instances.

\input{manuscript/table_aggregate_depth}

Figure~\ref{fig:depth-scaling} shows the same trend as a function of depth.
The complete/Grover mixer improves with \(p\), but remains below Johnson-type
mixers. The weighted Johnson mixer with \(\alpha=0.25\) performs comparably to,
and at depth \(p=3\) slightly better than, the unweighted Johnson mixer. This
suggests that including mild cost-landscape information in the feasible graph
can be useful, while overly strong weighting is not necessarily beneficial \gabriel{Given the size of the error bars, I'd hesitate to claim this}.

\subsection{Weighted Johnson graphs}

The weighted Johnson extension keeps the same feasible one-swap support as the
Johnson graph but changes the hopping amplitudes according to
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cost-weighted-johnson}. Table~\ref{tab:weighted-scan} and
Fig.~\ref{fig:weighted-alpha} summarize the scan over \(\alpha\). The best
aggregate result occurs for a small positive weight, \(\alpha=0.25\), while
large weights degrade performance. This is consistent with the interpretation
that a mild cost-sensitive deformation of the feasible walk can help guide the
search, whereas excessively suppressing transitions can reduce useful
exploration. \gabriel{The writing is a bit weird here. Results for weighted Johnson graphs were already discussed in the previous paragraph a lot.}

\subsection{Complete graph versus Grover mixer}

The complete-graph QWOA mixer and the Dicke+Grover mixer are analytically
equivalent up to a global phase and the parameter rescaling
\(\theta=M\beta/2\). Table~\ref{tab:grover-equivalence} verifies this relation
numerically for all feasible sizes used in the extended benchmark. The residual
errors are at floating-point precision, with a maximum Frobenius-norm error of
\(1.16\times 10^{-13}\). Therefore, any small mismatch between separately
optimized complete-graph and Dicke+Grover circuits should be interpreted as an
implementation or optimization effect, not as a difference between the ideal
mixers.

\subsection{Graph-level resources and implementation diagnostics}


The complete feasible graph has \(|\mathcal{F}|(|\mathcal{F}|-1)/2\) edges,
whereas the Johnson graph has
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{2}\binom{n}{k} k(n-k)
\end{equation}
edges and degree \(k(n-k)\). Weighted Johnson graphs have the same support as the Johnson graph and
therefore do not increase the number of graph edges at the abstract graph
level, although nonuniform weights may affect circuit-level implementation
costs. Representative values are shown in
Table~\ref{tab:graph-resources}. For example, for \((n,k)=(8,4)\), the complete
feasible graph has \(2415\) edges, while the Johnson and weighted-Johnson
graphs have \(560\) edges.

\input{manuscript/table_resources}

substantially deeper than the simple penalty-QAOA example. This does not prove
an intrinsic disadvantage of complete-graph mixing, but it emphasizes that
feasible-state preparation, reflection operations, and the hardware-level
